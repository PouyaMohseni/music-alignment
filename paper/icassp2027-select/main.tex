\begin{abstract}
Score following aligns a live performance to its score in real time,
committing to a position at every instant with no lookahead. It concerns two input
modalities, image and audio, and the established response is to convert one into the other and align within a
single modality, by optical music recognition or by transcription, each detour
inheriting the errors of its conversion. Sheet-image score following conditions a
visual detector on the audio, and what limits it is
annotation, since alignments to notehead coordinates are costly enough that
nearly all training data is synthesized and these systems lose much of their
accuracy on a real instrument in a real room. Every remedy proposed for that
gap has treated it as a failure of perception, yet we show that the detector
already proposes a position within the standard tolerance at every scored onset
of a real recording and that an oracle held to the same realtime commitments
recovers almost everything the deployed rule loses. We therefore
recast the task as sequential selection over the candidates the detector
already emits, combining a parameter-free transition model with a set-based
scorer that judges each candidate against its competitors. This sets a new best
on real piano recordings and shows that what the decision needs is its own
temporal history rather than a better image representation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Score following, sheet music, audio-to-score alignment, realtime,
music information retrieval
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Music alignment puts a performance and its score in correspondence, mapping
each moment of audio to a position in the written music. The regimes differ in
whether alignment may look ahead. Offline, known as audio-to-score
alignment or music synchronisation, the whole recording is in hand, dynamic
time warping settles every position at once~\cite{dixon2005match}, repeats and
jumps are recoverable after the fact~\cite{shan2020,morsi2022}, and the
correspondences support cross-modal retrieval and piece
identification~\cite{dorfer2018msmd}.

In the online version, however, the system observes only what has been played,
commits immediately and never revises. This is score
following~\cite{orio2003}, also called music tracking~\cite{arzt2010}, in which
the audio up to frame $t$ fixes an estimate of the performer's position that
must be produced before frame $t+1$ arrives. Automatic page
turning~\cite{arzt2008}, live computer
accompaniment~\cite{dannenberg1984,raphael2010} and score-synchronised display
depend on it, and every result below, the oracles included, respects the
constraint. The literature bridges the two modalities in
three ways, differing in which side, if either, is converted.

Using optical music recognition, the first category renders the page as
symbols, after which a classical follower tracks a discrete sequence of states,
a line running from online dynamic time warping~\cite{dixon2005match} through
duration models~\cite{cont2010}, hidden semi-Markov models~\cite{nakamura2015},
particle filters~\cite{korzeniowski2013}, switching state-space
models~\cite{jiang2020} and multi-agent tracking~\cite{arzt2010}. The second
category converts the audio instead, pairing realtime piano
transcription~\cite{kong2021} with symbol-level tracking to strong
effect~\cite{peter2025}, at the price of bounding the follower by the
transcriber.

The third category learns the audio-to-pixel correspondence directly. Dorfer et al.~\cite{dorfer2016} classified position into
staff-level buckets on crops; reinforcement learning framed tracking as an
agent on a score strip~\cite{dorfer2018rl,henkel2019rl};
CUNet~\cite{henkel2020cunet} emitted an audio-conditioned segmentation of the
page; and CYOLO~\cite{henkel2021eusipco} recast it as audio-conditioned object
detection, CYOLO-SB~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} adding heads for the enclosing
system and bar. CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} cascades system, bar and note
selection over layout regions with beam search and learned transition penalties
and is the strongest published system here, though it reads the page's system
and bar boxes at test time, which the others and ours do not. These methods
require performances aligned to notehead coordinates, an annotation costly
enough that nearly all training data is synthesized, so they learn on rendered
audio and lose much of their accuracy in a real room, CUNet falling
from 85.5\% of onsets within 0.5\,s to 22.4\%, CYOLO-SB from 89.3\% to 79.9\%
and CODA from 95.5\% to 88.3\%. Every remedy has been a perception remedy, whether reverberant augmentation~\cite{henkel2021eusipco}, extra
proprietary recordings~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} or a new
architecture~\cite{yang2026coda}.

An audio-conditioned detector does not output a position but a scored set of
candidate noteheads, of which the most confident is reported and the rest
discarded. What that set contains bounds what any selection rule could
achieve, and on the room-microphone recordings the bound lies far above what is
realised. Among the 256 highest-scoring candidates at each
instant, one falls within the 0.5\,s tolerance at all 4,149 scored onsets, and
a causal bound that must commit at each instant and never select a position
behind its last reaches 99.9\%, against 80.0\% for the deployed argmax
(Fig.~\ref{fig:example}). Almost all of the twenty points lost on real audio
are therefore recoverable under the realtime constraint, which locates the loss in
candidate ordering rather than in the visual evidence.

This argues for treating the task as a sequential decision over the positions a
page affords rather than the regression of a coordinate. \sys{} decides from a
transition model, scoring a displacement against elapsed time, and a set-based
scorer, judging each candidate against its competitors in the same instant,
trained listwise on synthesized audio alone and decoded causally and greedily
at 1.25\,ms per instant on one CPU thread. Thus we relocate the
synthetic-to-real gap from perception to the decision and act on it, setting a
new best on real recordings across three released detectors.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Backbone}
\label{ssec:backbone}

Every sheet-image follower shares a perception module,
a convolutional network over the score page whose feature maps are modulated by
the audio. The mechanism is feature-wise linear
modulation~\cite{perez2018film}, which injects a vector into a
network by scaling and shifting its activations,
$f_{\mathrm{FiLM}}(x)=s(z)\odot x+t(z)$, where $s$ and $t$ are learned linear
maps of a conditioning vector $z$ and $\odot$ is elementwise. As the page does not indicate where the performer is,
$z$ carries the recent audio and the visual features become a function of what
has just been heard.

CYOLO~\cite{henkel2021eusipco} encodes the last two seconds of audio into $z$
with a spectrogram network and places FiLM layers through the downscale and
upscale blocks of a YOLO detector~\cite{redmon2016yolo}, which then predicts a bounding box for the
notehead currently sounding. CYOLO-SB~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} keeps that
arrangement and adds heads for the enclosing system and bar.
CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} conditions its own backbone the same way, at the
deeper stages, before cascading selection over layout regions supplied with the
score. We use the released CYOLO detectors to propose noteheads.

\subsection{\sys{}}
\label{ssec:candor}

\sys{}, for candidate ordering, sits after the backbone and replaces only its
final step. At each instant the detector offers a few hundred possible notehead
positions, and where it would report the most confident of them, \sys{} asks
which one is most compatible with where its own previous decision was and with
what the rest of the set looks like. The two parts below formalise these
criteria, and each requires state to be carried across instants.

\subsubsection{Candidate positions}

A performance is processed in instants $t=1,\dots,T$, and at each one the
backbone reads the audio so far and the current page and scores note boxes
$b_i=(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i)$ with confidence $o_i$; we keep the $K=256$ most
confident, $\mathcal{C}_t$. Staves are concatenated along one horizontal axis,
the unrolled coordinate $u_i$, so positions across a line break are adjacent.
The released detectors report the most confident box,
$\hat{\imath}_t=\arg\max_{i} o_i$, the degenerate selector. \sys{} reads
$\mathcal{C}_t$ and its previous choice $\hat u_{t-1}$, and nothing else. $K$ is set where availability
stops binding, a correct candidate being present at every onset only from
$K=256$. \sys{} saturates earlier, reaching 89.8\%, 92.2\%, 94.1\%, 95.0\% and
94.4\% at $K=16$, 32, 64, 128 and 256, the last two within the seed
deviation.

\subsubsection{Transition model}

Between instants $\Delta_t$ frames apart the performer advances by a roughly
predictable amount, so the displacement $d_i=u_i-\hat u_{t-1}$ is scored by a floored Gaussian whose mean
grows with elapsed time,
\begin{equation}
  \log\pi_i=\max\Big(-\frac{(d_i-\mu\rho_t)^2}{2\sigma^2},\,J\Big),
  \label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_t=\mathrm{clip}(\Delta_t/\Delta_0,\,0.2,\,8)$ and $\Delta_0=5$
frames, the mean inter-onset gap in training. The floor $J$ keeps a jump
possible at bounded cost, and combined with confidence $\ell_i=\log o_i+\log\pi_i$
already recovers a large part of the gap.

\subsubsection{Set-based scorer}

The transition model scores each candidate independently given the history and
therefore cannot express a comparative judgement. What it omits is whether a
given box, relative to the others available at the same instant, is a plausible
notehead rather than a spurious response. That comparison matters on real
audio, where reverberation smears the onset the conditioning vector is built
from, so the detector answers with many moderately confident boxes rather than
one sharp response and absolute confidence is poorly calibrated. Each candidate
is described by $\phi_i\in\mathbb{R}^{33}$ holding its confidence relative to
the instant's best, box geometry, motion relative to the history, relation to
any coarse regions the backbone emits, and how crowded and how typical the box
is among its neighbours, together with $f_i$, the 128-dimensional
FiLM-conditioned feature the detection head scored the box from. A
permutation-invariant network~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}, as in learned
re-scoring of detections~\cite{hosang2017nms}, embeds each candidate as
$e_i=g([\phi_i;\,\mathrm{ReLU}(Wf_i)])$ and scores it against a context pooled
over the set, $s_i=r([e_i;\,c])$ with
$c=[\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_j e_j;\ \max_j e_j]$, the maximum taken per dimension.
Here $g$ and $r$ are two-layer perceptrons and
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{64\times128}$, so every candidate is judged against its
competitors.

\subsubsection{Training and inference}

The decision is supervised listwise against soft targets, so with
$\varepsilon_i$ the absolute difference in frames between the true onset time
and the time the score maps candidate $i$'s position to, which is the quantity
the evaluation thresholds, the target is
$q_i\propto\exp(-\varepsilon_i/\tau)$ and the loss is the cross-entropy
$-\sum_i q_i\log\mathrm{softmax}(s)_i$, under which the scorer learns to order
a set rather than regress a coordinate. History features come from previous
decisions, perturbed by Laplace noise whose heavy tail exposes training to both
drift and gross error~\cite{bengio2015scheduled}. Target width and perturbation
are chosen by ablation on the validation set: $\tau=3$ frames, about 0.15\,s,
rewards landing near the onset rather than exactly on it, and noise of scale
30\,px on 30\% of samples is worth 2.9 points over an unperturbed history,
which leaves the scorer below the argmax baseline it replaces; heavier noise is
worse. At inference \sys{} decides
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\imath}_t=\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t}\ \beta\,s_i+(1-\beta)\,\ell_i ,
  \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}
causally and greedily, one pass over 256 candidates per instant, in 1.25\,ms on
a single CPU thread against the 50\,ms available at 20\,fps. Training on the
residual $s_i-\ell_i$ and deciding $\arg\max_i(s_i+\ell_i)$ is Eq.~\ref{eq:decode} at
$\beta=0.5$ and eliminates all four constants.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

All experiments use MSMD~\cite{dorfer2018msmd}, which pairs
LilyPond engravings with MIDI-derived alignments for solo piano. Its repertoire
is Western classical, weighted towards the Baroque and Classical and dominated
by Bach, with Beethoven, Mozart, Handel, Schumann, Chopin, Czerny and
Burgm\"uller making up most of the remainder, and textures from two-voice
inventions to dense Romantic writing, which sets how many plausible candidates
a page affords at each instant.

The decision model is trained on 353 pieces, synthesized and convolved with
measured room impulse responses~\cite{traer2016}, and validated on 19, using
AdamW ($3\cdot10^{-3}$, cosine, 40 epochs, batch 256). Evaluation is the
real-performance benchmark of~\cite{henkel2019rl}, 16 pieces of Bach, Schumann,
Mozart, Chopin and Mussorgsky spanning 25 pages and 4,149 onsets, played on a
Yamaha AvantGrand N2 and captured with a room microphone, with the correct page
given throughout. A further 80 held-out pieces are rendered with a real impulse
response and noise from 12 to 0.5\,dB SNR. We report the percentage of onsets
whose predicted position maps to within 0.05 to 5\,s of the true time, 0.5\,s
primary, with 95\% intervals bootstrapped over pieces~\cite{efron1993} and seed
means with deviations.

The protocol separates three kinds of data across three stages, in that the
decision model is trained on synthesized audio alone and never sees a real
recording, the four constants
$(\mu,\sigma,J,\beta)$ are calibrated leave-one-piece-out on the other 15 real
pieces weighted equally, and evaluation is on the piece held out. That still
lets the benchmark as a whole participate in choosing them, which the published
baselines did not require, so the residual form of Eq.~\ref{eq:decode} is
carried alongside as the variant fitting nothing to any real recording.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig_analysis.pdf}
  \caption{(a) 80 held-out pieces under a room impulse response and additive
  noise, 40,594 onsets per level. (b) 48 trained decision models; half fall in
  the shaded 1.5-point validation band and differ by 19 points on real audio.}
  \label{fig:analysis}
\end{figure}

Since \sys{} orders proposals, it applies to any follower that produces a
candidate set and then discards it. CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} is not such a target, since
its final stage regresses a continuous bar-local coordinate rather than
choosing among proposals, and the sets it does select over, systems and bars,
are supplied by layout annotation and already decoded with beam search.

Table~\ref{tab:main} sets \sys{} against the published literature on
recordings played straight through, the last column giving the improvement at
0.5\,s over the perception model each row is built on.
\sys{} tracks 94.4\% of onsets within 0.5\,s, 95\% CI over pieces
$[92.2,\,97.1]\%$, against a previous best of 88.3\%, and leads at every
threshold. We acknowledge that sixteen pieces are few and that the interval is
correspondingly wide, though the gain over the argmax it replaces, paired on
the same resample, is $+7.9$ points with interval $[+2.8,\,+12.2]$. The margin does
not depend on one perception model either, since retraining the decision on
whichever candidates each detector emits gains 20.4, 10.7 and 7.9 points, and
on the weakest, whose training data is entirely public, it passes where the
strongest one begins. On 94
synthetic test pieces it reaches 95.5\% against 89.3\% for argmax, and on the
80 noisy held-out pieces it beats the transition model at every level from 12
to 0.5\,dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}a).

Since a selection rule is only as precise as the boxes it chooses between, the
gain concentrates at the tolerances that measure which notehead is active
rather than the position within it, and at 0.05 and 0.1\,s \sys{} reaches
69.0\% and 71.4\% on CYOLO-SB candidates against CODA's 72.1\% and 74.3\%. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} decomposes the gain over
the same candidates by adding Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} to the argmax and then the
scorer, with $n$ the number of decision-model seeds at a fixed detector, and
shows that the transition model alone, which has no learned parameters,
accounts for roughly half of it.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{ssec:abl}

Giving the scorer the FiLM-conditioned backbone feature, DINOv2 patch
features~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} on the same grid, or no image feature at all
yields 90.6\%, 90.9\% and 90.9\%, a spread well inside the 0.9-point seed deviation,
where on synthesized validation audio the same three differ by 3.4 points.
Removing the features computed against the previous decision instead costs 7.2
points, the largest single ablation here, and teacher forcing attributes two
thirds of the remaining error to drift, since given the true previous position
the same scorer reaches 98.0\% against the causal oracle's 99.9\%.

Retraining the detector from scratch with a different audio encoder
gives the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:main}, not comparable with the
converged checkpoints above it and reported because the decision gains a
similar margin on any of them. A frozen MERT-95M tower~\cite{li2024mert}
reaches 46.2\%, driving training loss down by a factor of 4.7 while validation
loss stays flat; the two-layer causal Mamba~\cite{gu2024mamba}
of~\cite{yang2026coda} in place of the whole audio tower reaches 76.2\% against
83.3\% for an identically budgeted control; and keeping the convolutional front
end while replacing only the recurrence, which we call CYOLO-Mamba, reaches
91.5\%. The recurrence therefore benefits from a state-space model while the
convolutional front end is load-bearing, the encoder ablation absent
from~\cite{yang2026coda}. None of this rescues the gap, the strongest of these
merely reaching what re-deciding over an already
released one achieves.

Across 48 trained models, accuracy on the synthesized validation split
correlates $r=0.16$ with accuracy on the real recordings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}b), and the best-validation model scores 89.3\%, below
one reading no image features at all. A reverberant held-out set correlates
more strongly ($r=0.80$) yet still selects poorly. Since the field selects
models on synthesized audio, this is a caution beyond our own experiments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The synthetic-to-real gap in sheet-image score following has been read as a
failure of perception and answered with better audio, more data and new
architectures. The measurement reported here places it elsewhere. On real
room-microphone recordings the detector already proposes a correct position at
every scored onset, and an oracle under the same realtime commitments
recovers almost all of what the deployed rule loses, so at the standard
tolerance what fails is the rule that reads a position from the evidence
rather than the evidence itself.

Acting on that, \sys{} orders the candidates the detector already emits and
takes real piano recordings from 80.0\% to 94.4\% of onsets within 0.5\,s,
against 88.3\% for the best published system, at 1.25\,ms per instant on one
CPU thread, without retraining any detector and without layout annotation, by a
margin holding across three released detectors. Within this framework the
decision depends far more on its own temporal history than on the image
representations we tested. Two results temper this, the first being that accuracy on synthesized audio
barely predicts accuracy on real audio across 48 trained models, which makes
selection on a synthetic proxy unreliable for the field and not only for us,
and the second that the forward-motion assumption of Eq.~\ref{eq:prior}
confines the method to performances played in the written order. Pitch agreement between a candidate
and the audio is the natural next source of evidence independent of the
history.
