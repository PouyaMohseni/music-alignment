\begin{abstract}
Score following aligns a live performance to its score in real time, committing
to a position at every instant with no lookahead. The two sides are different
kinds of object, the performance audio and the score an image. The
established response is to convert one into the other and align within a single
modality, either by turning the page into symbols with optical music
recognition or by turning the audio into notes with transcription, and each
detour inherits the errors of its conversion. Sheet-image score following
converts neither side and learns the correspondence directly, and its strongest
systems condition a visual detector on the audio. What limits them is
annotation, since performances aligned to notehead coordinates are costly
enough that nearly all training data is synthesized, so these systems work on
synthesized audio and lose much of their accuracy on a real instrument in a
real room. Closing that gap is the field's central open problem, and every
remedy proposed for it has treated the gap as a failure of perception. We
present evidence that it is not, since on real recordings the detector already
proposes a position within the standard tolerance at every scored onset, and an
oracle held to the same realtime commitments recovers almost everything the
deployed rule loses. We therefore recast the task as sequential selection over
the candidates the detector already emits,
combining a parameter-free transition model with a set-based scorer that judges
each candidate against its competitors in the same instant. This sets a new
best on real piano recordings played straight through, holds across three
released detectors that differ in heads and in training data, and shows that
what the decision needs is its own temporal history rather than a better image
representation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Score following, sheet music, audio-to-score alignment, realtime,
music information retrieval
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Score following~\cite{orio2003} is the online alignment of an acoustic signal
to the score being performed. Given the audio observed up to frame $t$, the
system estimates the position the performer currently occupies, and must do so
before frame $t+1$ arrives. It supports automatic page turning~\cite{arzt2008},
live computer accompaniment~\cite{dannenberg1984,dannenberg2006,raphael2010}
and score-synchronised visualisation.

Offline alignment, or music synchronisation, is a different problem. The whole
recording is available, a global method such as dynamic time warping settles
every position at once~\cite{dixon2005match}, repeats and jumps are resolved
after the fact~\cite{shan2020,morsi2022}, and the result underpins cross-modal
retrieval and piece identification~\cite{dorfer2018msmd}. Score following, also
called music tracking~\cite{arzt2010}, has none of these advantages, and
everything below respects that constraint, the oracles included.

Its difficulty is that the two sides are different kinds of object. The
performance is audio and the score an image, with no shared space in which to
compare them, a mismatch the literature answers in three ways.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{ssec:related}

Two families make the sides commensurable by converting one of them.
Converting the score, optical music recognition turns the page into symbols so
that a classical follower can track over a discrete set of states, a
well-developed line spanning online dynamic time warping~\cite{dixon2005match},
duration-focused probabilistic models~\cite{cont2010}, hidden semi-Markov
models absorbing performer errors and arbitrary repeats~\cite{nakamura2015},
particle filters for rests and tempo change~\cite{korzeniowski2013}, switching
state-space models with hidden tempo~\cite{jiang2020} and multi-agent
tracking~\cite{arzt2010}; its weakness is that optical music recognition
remains error-prone on real scores~\cite{calvo2020omr} and the follower
inherits every mistake. Converting the audio instead, recent work pairs
realtime piano transcription with symbol-level tracking to strong
effect~\cite{peter2025}, though the follower is then bounded by the transcriber
and restricted to instruments transcription handles well.

The third family converts neither side and learns the correspondence between
audio and pixels directly. Dorfer et al.~\cite{dorfer2016} classified position
into staff-level buckets on crops; reinforcement learning framed tracking as an
agent navigating a score strip~\cite{dorfer2018rl,henkel2019rl};
CUNet~\cite{henkel2020cunet} moved to full pages with an audio-conditioned
segmentation whose centre of mass gives the position; and
CYOLO~\cite{henkel2021eusipco} recast it as audio-conditioned object detection,
CYOLO-SB~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} adding heads for the enclosing system and
bar. The concurrent CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} cascades system, bar and note
selection over layout regions with beam search and learned transition
penalties, and is the strongest published system on this benchmark, though it
reads the system and bar boxes of the page at test time, which the other
methods and ours do not.

All of them need performances aligned to notehead coordinates, an annotation
costly enough that nearly all of it is synthesized, a score rendered to MIDI
and the MIDI to audio. The result is systems that work on synthesized audio and
lose much of their accuracy on a real instrument in a real room,
CUNet~\cite{henkel2020cunet} falling from 85.5\% of onsets tracked within
0.5\,s to 22.4\%, CYOLO-SB~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} from 89.3\% to 79.9\% and
CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} from 95.5\% to 88.3\%. Closing that gap is the field's
central open problem, and every remedy proposed for it has been a perception
remedy, whether reverberant augmentation~\cite{henkel2021eusipco}, extra
proprietary recordings~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} or a new
architecture~\cite{yang2026coda}.

\subsection{Diagnosis}
\label{ssec:diagnosis}

We tested that premise before accepting it. A conditional detector does not
produce a position directly but scores thousands of candidate noteheads and
reports the most confident. Keeping the 256 most confident, on real
room-microphone recordings a candidate within the 0.5\,s tolerance is present
at every one of 4,149 scored onsets, so an oracle free to pick any of them is
perfect but could not be run live. The oracle that matters commits at each
instant and never moves backwards, exactly as a deployed follower must, and
still reaches 99.6\% where the deployed rule reaches 80.0\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:example}). Nineteen of the twenty points lost to real audio
survive the realtime constraint. At this tolerance, then, reverberation
degrades the detector's confidence ordering more than the visual evidence
beneath it, and Sec.~\ref{sec:results} qualifies how far that holds as the
tolerance tightens.

We therefore recast the task as a sequential decision over the positions the
page affords rather than the regression of a coordinate. \sys{} decides with a
transition model scoring a displacement against elapsed time and a set-based
scorer judging each candidate against its competitors in the same instant,
trained listwise on synthesized audio alone, causally and greedily, at
1.25\,ms per instant on one CPU thread against the 50\,ms available at
20\,frames per second.

Our contribution is to relocate the synthetic-to-real gap in this task and to
act on it. Candidate-set oracles place it in the decision rather than in
perception, and \sys{}, a decision model built on that finding, sets a new best
on real recordings by a margin holding across three released detectors
differing in heads and in training data.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Backbone}
\label{ssec:backbone}

Every sheet-image follower of the last five years shares one perception module,
a convolutional network over the score page whose feature maps are modulated by
the audio. The mechanism is feature-wise linear
modulation~\cite{perez2018film}, which injects an external vector into a
network by scaling and shifting its activations,
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{FiLM}}(x)=s(z)\odot x+t(z),
  \label{eq:film}
\end{equation}
where $s$ and $t$ are learned linear maps of a conditioning vector $z$ and
$\odot$ is elementwise. The page alone cannot say where the performer is, so
$z$ carries the recent audio and the visual features become a function of what
has just been heard.

CYOLO~\cite{henkel2021eusipco} encodes the last two seconds of audio into $z$
with a spectrogram network and places FiLM layers through the downscale and
upscale blocks of a YOLO detector~\cite{redmon2016yolo}, which then predicts a bounding box for the
notehead currently sounding. CYOLO-SB~\cite{henkel2021frontiers} keeps that
arrangement and adds heads for the enclosing system and bar, so one 
$52\times52$ feature map yields all three. CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} conditions
its own backbone the same way, at the deeper stages, before cascading selection
over layout regions supplied with the score.

We take this module as given rather than as a design choice, and use the
released CYOLO detectors to propose noteheads. No detector weight is updated
anywhere in this work, and three released instances are used, differing in
their heads and in their training data, so the conclusions concern the family
and not one checkpoint.

\subsection{\sys{}}
\label{ssec:candor}

\sys{}, for candidate ordering, sits after the backbone and replaces only its
final step. At each instant the detector offers a few hundred possible notehead
positions, and where it would report the most confident of them, \sys{} asks
which one is most compatible with where its own previous decision was and with
what the rest of the set looks like. Those two questions are the two parts
below, and answering them requires carrying state from one instant to the next.

\subsubsection{Candidate positions}

A performance is processed in instants $t=1,\dots,T$, and at each one the
backbone reads the audio so far and the current page and scores note boxes
$b_i=(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i)$ with confidence $o_i$; we keep the $K=256$ most
confident, $\mathcal{C}_t$. Staves are concatenated along one horizontal axis,
the unrolled coordinate $u_i$, so positions across a line break are adjacent.
The released detectors score 8,112 boxes per instant and report
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\imath}_t=\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t} o_i ,
  \label{eq:argmax}
\end{equation}
which is the degenerate selector. \sys{} reads $\mathcal{C}_t$ and its own
previous choice $\hat u_{t-1}$, and nothing else. $K$ is set where
availability stops binding, since a correct candidate is present at every
onset only from 256 and the causal oracle falls to 85.6\% at $K=64$, below
what \sys{} itself reaches, so a smaller set would limit the decision rather
than test it.

\subsubsection{Transition model}

Selections must form a plausible trajectory, and between instants $\Delta_t$
frames apart the performer advances by a roughly predictable amount, so the
displacement $d_i=u_i-\hat u_{t-1}$ is scored by a floored Gaussian whose mean
grows with elapsed time,
\begin{equation}
  \log\pi_i=\max\Big(-\frac{(d_i-\mu\rho_t)^2}{2\sigma^2},\,J\Big),
  \label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_t=\mathrm{clip}(\Delta_t/\Delta_0,\,0.2,\,8)$ and $\Delta_0=5$
frames, the mean inter-onset gap in training. The floor $J$ keeps a jump
possible at bounded cost. Combined with confidence, $h_i=\log o_i+\log\pi_i$
already recovers a large part of the gap on its own.

\subsubsection{Set-based scorer}

The transition model treats candidates independently given the history, so what
it cannot express is comparative, whether this box, among those on offer in
this instant, looks like a real notehead rather than a spurious response. This
is what real audio in particular calls for, since reverberation smears the
onset the conditioning vector is built from and the detector answers with many
moderately confident boxes rather than one sharp response, so absolute
confidence is poorly calibrated while the shape of the set is still
informative. Each
candidate is described by $\phi_i\in\mathbb{R}^{33}$ holding its confidence
relative to the instant's best, box geometry, motion relative to the history,
relation to any coarse regions the backbone emits, and a neighbourhood group
describing how crowded and how typical the box is among its neighbours. We also
read $f_i$, the 128-dimensional FiLM-conditioned feature from which the
detection head scored the box, so the decision sees the representation
perception used. A permutation-invariant network~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}, as
in learned re-scoring of detections~\cite{hosang2017nms}, embeds the set and
pools a context shared by all candidates,
\begin{align}
  e_i &= g\big([\phi_i;\,\mathrm{ReLU}(Wf_i)]\big),\quad
  c=\Big[\tfrac{1}{K}{\textstyle\sum_j} e_j;\ \max_j e_j\Big],
  \label{eq:embed}\\
  s_i &= r\big([e_i;\,c]\big),
  \label{eq:score}
\end{align}
with $g$ and $r$ two-layer perceptrons (64, 32 and 64, 1) and
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{64\times128}$. The maximum is taken per dimension, so every
candidate is judged against its competitors.

\subsubsection{Training and inference}

The decision is supervised listwise, so with $\varepsilon_i$ the error in frames
of candidate $i$ the target is $q_i\propto\exp(-\varepsilon_i/\tau)$, $\tau=3$,
and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t} q_i\,\log\frac{\exp s_i}{\sum_{j}\exp s_j},
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
so the scorer learns to order a set rather than to regress a coordinate.
History features come from previous decisions, perturbed on 30\% of samples by
Laplace noise of scale 30\,px to expose training to the drift of sequence
prediction~\cite{bengio2015scheduled}. At inference \sys{} decides
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\imath}_t=\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t}\ \beta\,s_i+(1-\beta)\,h_i ,
  \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}
causally and greedily, one pass over 256 candidates per instant, in 1.25\,ms on
a single CPU thread against the 50\,ms available at 20\,fps. Training on the
residual $s_i-h_i$ and deciding $\arg\max_i(s_i+h_i)$ is Eq.~\ref{eq:decode} at
$\beta=0.5$ and eliminates all four constants.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

All experiments use MSMD~\cite{dorfer2018msmd}, which pairs public-domain
LilyPond engravings with MIDI-derived alignments for solo piano. Its repertoire
is Western classical, weighted towards the Baroque and Classical periods and
dominated by Bach, with Beethoven, Mozart, Handel, Schumann, Chopin, Czerny and
Burgm\"uller making up most of the remainder. Textures range from two-voice
inventions to dense Romantic writing, which matters here because the number of
simultaneous noteheads sets how many plausible candidates a page affords at
each instant.

The decision model is trained on 353 pieces, synthesized and convolved with
measured room impulse responses~\cite{traer2016}, and validated on 19, using
AdamW ($3\cdot10^{-3}$, cosine, 40 epochs, batch 256). Evaluation is the
real-performance benchmark of~\cite{henkel2019rl}, 16 pieces of Bach, Schumann,
Mozart, Chopin and Mussorgsky spanning 25 pages and 4,149 onsets, played on a
Yamaha AvantGrand N2 and captured with a room microphone. A further 80 held-out
pieces are rendered with a real impulse response and noise from 12 to 0.5\,dB
SNR. We report the percentage of onsets whose predicted position maps to within
0.05 to 5\,s of the true time, 0.5\,s primary, with 95\% intervals bootstrapped
over pieces~\cite{efron1993}, and seed means with deviations.

Three kinds of data enter at three stages and are worth keeping apart. The
decision model is trained on synthesized audio alone and never sees a real
recording. The four constants $(\mu,\sigma,J,\beta)$ are calibrated on real
recordings leave-one-piece-out, chosen for each evaluation piece on the other
15 with pieces weighted equally. Evaluation is on the piece held out. Leaving a
piece out of its own constants does not stop the benchmark as a whole from
participating in choosing them, which the published baselines did not require,
so the residual form of Eq.~\ref{eq:decode} is carried alongside as the variant
that fits nothing to any real recording.

\sys{} orders proposals, so it applies to a follower that produces a candidate
set and then discards it. CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} is not such a target, since
its final stage regresses a continuous bar-local coordinate rather than
choosing among proposals, and the sets it does select over, systems and bars,
are supplied by layout annotation and already decoded with beam search. It
appears in Table~\ref{tab:main} as the strongest published system on these
recordings, not as a backbone \sys{} could be attached to.

Table~\ref{tab:main} sets \sys{} against the published literature, taken
from~\cite{yang2026coda}, on recordings played straight through;
Sec.~\ref{ssec:jump} reports what happens when a performance is not, and
$\Delta$ is the gain at 0.5\,s over the perception model each row is built on.
\sys{} tracks 94.4\% of onsets within 0.5\,s, 95\% CI over pieces
$[92.2,\,97.1]$, against a previous best of 88.3\%, and leads at every
threshold. Sixteen pieces are few and the interval is correspondingly wide,
though the gain over the argmax it replaces, paired on the same resample, is
$+7.9$ with interval $[+2.8,\,+12.2]$. Nor is the margin an artifact of one
perception model. Retrained on whichever candidates each emits, the decision
gains 20.4, 10.7 and 7.9 points, and on the weakest, whose training data is
entirely public, it passes where the strongest one begins. A decision model
does not need good proposals, only proposals containing the answer. On 94
synthetic test pieces it reaches 95.5\% against 89.3\% for argmax, and on the
80 noisy held-out pieces it beats the transition model at every level from 12
to 0.5\,dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}a).

The tightest thresholds qualify this. On CYOLO-SB candidates \sys{} reaches
69.0\% and 71.4\% at 0.05 and 0.1\,s, below CODA's 72.1\% and 74.3\%, since a
decision is only as precise as the boxes it chooses between and the mechanism
recovers which notehead is active rather than the position within it.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} separates the parts over the same candidates, adding
Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} to Eq.~\ref{eq:argmax} and then the scorer, with $n$
counting decision-model seeds at a fixed detector. The transition model alone,
with no learned parameters, accounts for roughly half the gain. The residual
variant is the one to read for the strictest protocol, fitting no constant to
any real recording and so comparable with the published rows on equal terms; it
reaches 88.7\% against the 80.0\% it replaces, above the previous best, and the
distance from there to 94.4\% is what calibrating four constants is worth.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{ssec:abl}

Giving the scorer the FiLM-conditioned backbone feature, DINOv2 patch
features~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} on the same grid, or no image feature at all
yields 90.6, 90.9 and 90.9, a spread well inside the 0.9-point seed deviation,
where on synthesized validation audio the same three differ by 3.4 points. Those
features are learnable and useful there and worth nothing on real audio.
Removing the features computed against the previous decision instead costs 7.2
points, the largest single ablation here, and teacher forcing attributes two
thirds of the remaining error to drift, since given the true previous position
the same scorer reaches 98.0\% against the causal oracle's 99.6\%.

We also rebuilt the perception model, retraining the detector from scratch for
a fixed 24-hour budget with a different audio encoder. These are the lower
block of Table~\ref{tab:main}, not comparable with the converged checkpoints
above it and reported because the decision gains a similar margin whatever it
is attached to. A frozen MERT-95M tower~\cite{li2024mert} with only a
projection trained reaches 46.2 and overfits 12.6-fold. Replacing the whole
audio tower with the two-layer causal Mamba~\cite{gu2024mamba} of~\cite{yang2026coda}, trained as
they train it, reaches 76.2 against 83.3 for an identically budgeted control,
while keeping the convolutional front end and replacing only the recurrence,
which we call CYOLO-Mamba, reaches 91.5. The recurrence therefore benefits from
a state-space model and the convolutional front end is load-bearing, which is
the encoder ablation absent from~\cite{yang2026coda}. Replacing the visual stem
with frozen DINOv2-base patches on the same $52\times52$ grid, against a
control retrained identically, gives \tbd{.}. None of this rescues the gap on
its own, the strongest of these detectors merely reaching what re-deciding over
an already released one achieves.

Across 48 trained models, accuracy on the synthesized validation split
correlates $r=0.16$ with accuracy on the real recordings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}b), and the best-validation model scores 89.3 on real
audio, below one reading no image features at all. A reverberant held-out set
correlates more strongly ($r=0.80$) yet still selects poorly. Since the whole
field selects models on synthesized audio, this is a caution beyond our own
experiments, and it is why our constants are cross-validated on the target
recordings instead.

\subsection{Score discontinuities}
\label{ssec:jump}

Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} assumes the performance moves forward, which is what makes
the decision tractable and also what a repeat or a jump breaks. Splicing three
discontinuities into each performance at random destinations following the
protocol of~\cite{yang2026coda}, re-running perception on the spliced audio so
its conditioning is genuinely wrong afterwards and excluding jumps across a
page boundary where state resets anyway, 41 remain. Over the five seconds after
one, \sys{} tracks 8\% of onsets against 27\% for the argmax it replaces,
because the transition model holds the trajectory at a stale position.
Triggering a reset on the scorer's own confidence recovers this to 26\% and
costs 1.0 point on the unspliced recordings, so selection and discontinuity
detection compose at a stated price rather than contend. We report \sys{}
without the trigger, the evaluation repertoire being played straight through.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The synthetic-to-real gap in sheet-image score following has been read as a
failure of perception and answered with better audio, more data and new
architectures. The measurement reported here places it elsewhere. On real
room-microphone recordings the detector already proposes a correct position at
every scored onset, and an oracle held to the same realtime commitments
recovers almost all of what the deployed rule loses. At the standard
tolerance, then, most of what is needed to follow the performance survives the
room, and what fails is the rule that reads a position out of it.

Acting on that, \sys{} orders the candidates the detector already emits and
takes real piano recordings from 80.0\% to 94.4\% of onsets within 0.5\,s,
against 88.3\% for the best published system on a repertoire played straight
through, at 1.25\,ms per instant on a single CPU thread. The margin holds across
three released detectors differing in heads and in training data, which suggests
the finding concerns the family of audio-conditioned detectors rather than one
checkpoint, and it is obtained without retraining any of them and without layout
annotation at test time.

Within this framework the decision depends far more on its own temporal history
than on the image representations we tested, since three image representations including none at all give
the same real-audio accuracy while removing the history costs more than any
other ablation we ran.

Comparing a decoder of CODA's kind with ours over one shared candidate set is
a worthwhile experiment and a different one. Two results temper what is claimed here. Accuracy on synthesized audio barely predicts accuracy on real audio
across 48 trained models, which makes model selection on a synthetic proxy
unreliable for the whole field and not only for us. And the forward-motion
assumption that makes the decision tractable fails at a repeat, where \sys{} is
worse than the rule it replaces. As for what comes next, pitch agreement between a candidate and the audio is the natural source of
evidence independent of the history.
