\begin{abstract}
We formulate real-time score following on sheet-music images as sequential
selection: at each instant the page affords a set of note hypotheses, and
following the performance means choosing among them under a temporal
constraint, rather than regressing a coordinate. \sys{} realizes this with
three parts, a candidate generator, a parameter-free transition prior over
displacements, and a 23k-parameter permutation-invariant scorer trained
listwise on synthesized audio. On real room-microphone piano recordings \sys{}
tracks 94.4\% of onsets within 0.5\,s and runs in 1.25\,ms per step on one CPU
thread. The formulation explains where accuracy on real audio is lost. A
candidate oracle shows that a correct hypothesis is always present and a causal
selector could reach 99.6\%, so the synthetic-to-real gap is a decision
problem, not a perception problem; consistent with this, three different image
representations for the scorer, including a self-supervised vision transformer
and none at all, give identical accuracy, whereas removing the temporal history
costs 7.2 points. Because selection is separable from proposal, one trained
selector improves every candidate generator we attach it to, by 20.3, 11.0 and
7.9 points. We further report that accuracy on synthesized validation audio is
nearly uninformative about accuracy on real audio ($r=0.16$ over 48
checkpoints), and that the forward-motion prior which makes selection work is
also what makes score discontinuities hard.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Score following, sheet music, real-time alignment, set prediction,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Score following, the real-time alignment of a live performance to its score,
underlies automatic page turning~\cite{arzt2008} and computer
accompaniment~\cite{dannenberg1984,raphael2010}. Most music exists only as
printed or scanned sheets, and optical music recognition remains
error-prone~\cite{calvo2020omr}, so a line of work follows the performance
directly on the sheet image~\cite{dorfer2016,dorfer2018rl,henkel2019rl,%
henkel2020cunet,henkel2021eusipco,henkel2021frontiers,yang2026coda}. These
systems are trained on audio synthesized from the score and lose a large part
of their accuracy on real recordings, which is the field's main open problem.

Almost all of this work treats the task as \emph{regression}: a network maps
audio and a page to a coordinate, whether as a heatmap, a bounding box or a
reinforcement-learning action. We argue that the natural formulation is
\emph{selection}. A page of music affords a finite, discrete set of places the
performance could be at, one per notehead. Following the score is choosing
among them, again at every instant, subject to the constraint that the choices
form a plausible trajectory. Regression asks a network to synthesize a number
that a set already contains.

The distinction is not cosmetic, and one measurement makes it concrete. Given a
set of 256 note hypotheses per instant, we find that on real room-microphone
piano recordings a hypothesis within the 0.5\,s tolerance is present at every
one of 4,149 scored onsets, and that an oracle which must commit one step at a
time and may never move backwards tracks 99.6\% of them. A tracker built on the
same hypotheses that reports the most confident one tracks 80.0\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:example}). Nineteen of the twenty points lost to real audio are
lost in the decision, not in the perception. Under a regression view this is
invisible, because there is no set to take an oracle over.

\sys{} is a score follower built on this formulation. Its three parts are a
candidate generator, a transition prior with no trained parameters, and a small
permutation-invariant scorer that judges each hypothesis against the others in
its instant. Because selection is separable from proposal, the generator is an
interchangeable component: we attach the same trained selector to three
different published note detectors and it improves each of them, which is a
property of the formulation rather than a tuning result.

Our contributions are: (i)~a formulation of sheet-image score following as
sequential selection over note hypotheses, with a candidate-oracle analysis
that locates the synthetic-to-real gap in the decision; (ii)~\sys{}, a tracker
realizing it, which reaches 94.4\% of onsets within 0.5\,s on real recordings
at 1.25\,ms per step and transfers unchanged across candidate generators;
(iii)~three findings the formulation exposes and regression hides: the image
representation the selector reads is irrelevant while its temporal history is
essential, synthetic validation accuracy barely ranks models for real audio,
and the forward-motion prior that makes selection work is what makes
discontinuities hard.

\textbf{Related work.} Symbolic score followers already select, over score
states, with hidden (semi-)Markov models~\cite{cont2010,nakamura2015} or online
time warping~\cite{dixon2005match}; our formulation brings that stance to the
sheet image, where the state set must be proposed from pixels rather than read
from a symbolic score. Sheet-image trackers regress: staff
strips~\cite{dorfer2016}, reinforcement-learning
agents~\cite{dorfer2018rl,henkel2019rl}, full-page
segmentation~\cite{henkel2020cunet}, and audio-conditioned
detection~\cite{henkel2021eusipco,henkel2021frontiers} with
FiLM~\cite{perez2018film}, whose box proposals we reuse as a candidate
generator. The concurrent CODA~\cite{yang2026coda} also selects, over
annotated system and bar regions with a cascade trained end to end, and
contributes a discontinuity benchmark we adopt in Sec.~\ref{ssec:jump}; we
select over note hypotheses the generator already emits, so nothing is
retrained and no layout annotation is read. Set-based scoring
follows~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}, and re-scoring detections
has precedent in vision~\cite{hosang2017nms,hu2018relation}. Conditioning on
its own past choices exposes the selector to the drift of sequence
prediction~\cite{bengio2015scheduled,ross2011dagger}, which we quantify.

\section{Score following as sequential selection}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Hypotheses}

A performance is processed in instants $t=1,\dots,T$. At each instant a
candidate generator reads the audio so far and the current page and emits note
hypotheses $b_i=(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i)$ with confidence $o_i$; we keep the $K=256$
most confident, $\mathcal{C}_t$. Staves are concatenated along one horizontal
axis, the unrolled coordinate $u_i$, so that positions on either side of a line
break are adjacent. Any note detector can serve; we use released
ones~\cite{henkel2021eusipco,henkel2021frontiers}, which emit 8,112 hypotheses
per instant on a $52\times52$ grid, and never update their weights. Reporting
the most confident hypothesis,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\imath}_t=\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t} o_i ,
  \label{eq:argmax}
\end{equation}
is the degenerate selector, and is what a detection-based regressor does.
\sys{} reads $\mathcal{C}_t$ and its own previous choice
$\hat u_{t-1}=u_{\hat\imath_{t-1}}$, and nothing else.

\subsection{Temporal constraint}

Selections must form a plausible trajectory. Between instants $\Delta_t$ frames
apart the performer advances by a roughly predictable amount, so we score the
displacement $d_i=u_i-\hat u_{t-1}$ with a floored Gaussian whose mean grows
with elapsed time,
\begin{equation}
  \log\pi_i=\max\Big(-\frac{(d_i-\mu\rho_t)^2}{2\sigma^2},\,J\Big),
  \label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_t=\mathrm{clip}(\Delta_t/\Delta_0,\,0.2,\,8)$ and $\Delta_0=5$
frames, the mean inter-onset gap in training. The floor $J$ leaves jumps
possible at bounded cost. None of $(\mu,\sigma,J)$ is learned. Combined with
confidence, $h_i=\log o_i+\log\pi_i$, this alone is a competent selector
(Table~\ref{tab:transfer}).

\subsection{Set scorer}

The prior treats hypotheses independently given the history. What it cannot
express is comparative: whether \emph{this} box, among the ones on offer right
now, looks like a real notehead. Each hypothesis is therefore described by
$\phi_i\in\mathbb{R}^{33}$ holding confidence relative to the instant's best,
box geometry, motion relative to the history, relation to any coarse regions
the generator emits, and a neighbourhood group (how many hypotheses lie within
25 and 100\,px, spacing to the nearest on each side, rank in reading order,
size against the instant's median). We also read the 128-dimensional feature
$f_i$ from which the generator scored the box. A permutation-invariant
network~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets} embeds the set and pools a context every
hypothesis sees:
\begin{align}
  e_i &= g\big([\phi_i;\,\mathrm{ReLU}(Wf_i)]\big),\qquad
  c=\Big[\tfrac{1}{K}{\textstyle\sum_j} e_j;\ \max_j e_j\Big],
  \label{eq:embed}\\
  s_i &= r\big([e_i;\,c]\big),
  \label{eq:score}
\end{align}
with $g$ and $r$ two-layer perceptrons (64, 32 and 64, 1 units) and
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{64\times128}$. The maximum is per dimension, so each
hypothesis is judged against its competitors. The scorer has 23,203 parameters.

\subsection{Training and decoding}

Selection is supervised listwise. With $\varepsilon_i$ the error in frames of
hypothesis $i$, the target is $q_i\propto\exp(-\varepsilon_i/\tau)$, $\tau=3$,
and the loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t} q_i\,\log\frac{\exp s_i}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_t}\exp s_j},
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
so the scorer is trained to rank a set rather than to regress a coordinate.
Training runs on the MSMD training pieces~\cite{dorfer2018msmd}, synthesized and
convolved with measured room impulse responses~\cite{traer2016}. History
features come from the previous instants' selections, perturbed on 30\% of
samples by Laplace noise of scale 30\,px. We use AdamW ($3\cdot10^{-3}$, cosine,
40 epochs, batch 256). At each instant \sys{} selects
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\imath}_t=\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{C}_t}\ \beta\,s_i+(1-\beta)\,h_i ,
  \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}
and $\hat u_t$ becomes the history of instant $t+1$. Decoding is causal and
greedy: one forward pass over 256 hypotheses, 1.25\,ms on a single CPU thread.
Training the scorer on the residual $s_i-h_i$ and selecting
$\arg\max_i(s_i+h_i)$ is Eq.~\ref{eq:decode} at $\beta=0.5$ and removes all
four scalars from the system; that variant is reported below.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\textbf{Data and metric.} Selectors are trained on 353 MSMD
pieces~\cite{dorfer2018msmd} and validated on 19. The evaluation set is 16
pieces, 25 pages and 4,149 onsets played on a Yamaha AvantGrand N2 and captured
with a room microphone~\cite{henkel2019rl}. A held-out set of 80 further
pieces is rendered with a real impulse response and additive noise from 12 to
0.5\,dB SNR (40,594 onsets per level). We report the ratio of onsets whose
selected position maps to within 0.05 to 5\,s of the true time, 0.5\,s being
the primary figure, with 95\% intervals bootstrapped over
pieces~\cite{efron1993}.

\textbf{Selection of the four scalars.} $(\mu,\sigma,J,\beta)$ are fitted
leave-one-piece-out: for each evaluation piece they are chosen on the other 15
with pieces weighted equally, and that piece is decoded with them, so no piece
influences its own decoding. Every \sys{} number below is cross-validated this
way, and the residual variant needs no such fit at all. Seeds move results by
about a point, so rows are seed means with standard deviations.

\subsection{Selection is separable from proposal}
\label{ssec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports \sys{} on real recordings and
Table~\ref{tab:transfer} decomposes it. The same formulation, with the selector
retrained on whichever hypotheses the generator emits, holds across three
generators of very different quality. It is worth 20.3 points on the weakest,
whose training data is entirely public, lifting it past where the strongest
generator begins, and reaches 94.4\% on the strongest. A selector fitted to one
kind of proposal does not need the proposals to be good; it needs them to
contain the answer, which the oracle says they do.

Two rows keep the claim honest. The parameter-free prior alone accounts for
roughly half the gain, so the learned component is not carrying the result by
itself, and the residual variant, which fits no scalar to any evaluation data,
reaches 88.7\%. Selection is also cheap: 23k parameters and 1.25\,ms per
instant against 12.8\,ms on a GPU for the closest comparable
system~\cite{yang2026coda}.

\subsection{What selection needs, and what it does not}
\label{ssec:abl}

\textbf{Not pixels.} Feeding the selector the generator's backbone features,
DINOv2 patch features~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} at the same grid, or no image
features at all gives 90.9 in all three cases. On synthesized validation audio
the same three differ by 3.4 points, so the features are learnable and useful
there and worth nothing where it counts. Replacing the generator's audio
encoder is no better: a MERT tower~\cite{li2024mert} costs more than 40 points,
and \tbd{Mamba pending}. Perception is not the binding constraint, which is
what the oracle predicted.

\textbf{But history.} Removing the features computed against the previous
selection costs 7.2 points, the largest single ablation here. Teacher forcing
attributes about two thirds of the remaining error to drift: supplied the true
previous position the same selector reaches 98.0\% against the causal oracle's
99.6\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}a). Selection is sequential, and it is the
sequence that is hard.

\textbf{Synthetic accuracy does not rank selectors.} Across 48 trained
checkpoints, accuracy on synthesized validation audio correlates $r=0.16$ with
accuracy on real recordings; the best-validation checkpoint scores 89.3 on real
audio, below a selector reading no image features at all. A reverberant
held-out set does better ($r=0.80$) and still mispicks. This is why the four
scalars are fitted on the target recordings under cross-validation rather than
on a synthetic proxy, and it is a caution for the field.

\subsection{Where the temporal constraint hurts}
\label{ssec:jump}

Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} assumes forward motion, which is why selection works and
also why a repeat or a jump breaks it. We splice three discontinuities into
each performance at random destinations, following the protocol
of~\cite{yang2026coda}, re-running the generator on the spliced audio so its
conditioning is genuinely wrong afterwards, and excluding jumps across a page
boundary where our state resets anyway, leaving 41.

Table~\ref{tab:jump} reports a failure. \sys{} is worse after a jump than
selecting by confidence alone, 0.15 against 0.22 of onsets tracked over the
next five seconds, because the prior pins the trajectory to a stale position.
Suppressing the prior on detected silence recovers part of it when a break
exists and nothing when it does not; triggering instead on the selector's own
confidence, which needs no break, works in both cases but only returns to the
confidence baseline. A prior strong enough to be worth eleven points on
continuous playing is strong enough to be wrong at a discontinuity, and
handling both within one selector is open.

\subsection{Robustness and budget}

On held-out pieces (Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}b) the scorer improves on the prior
at every noise level, by less as the SNR falls: comparative evidence helps most
while the hypotheses are still trustworthy. On 94 synthetic test pieces, where
confidence alone selects .893 at 0.5\,s, \sys{} reaches .955. Halving the
hypothesis budget to $K=128$ costs 0.5 points, and the correct hypothesis sits
at rank 27 at the 90th percentile, so the tail of the set is load-bearing.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Posing sheet-image score following as sequential selection over note hypotheses
makes the synthetic-to-real gap measurable and mostly closable. The right
answer is already in the set on real audio; a 23k-parameter selector with a
parameter-free temporal prior finds it 94.4\% of the time at 1.25\,ms per
instant, and transfers across candidate generators because selection and
proposal are separate concerns. What the selector needs is its own history, not
better pixels, and the temporal constraint it depends on is exactly what fails
at a discontinuity. As in prior work the correct page is given, all performances
are piano, and with 16 evaluation pieces differences of a point or two are not
resolvable. Agreement between the pitches a hypothesis implies and those heard
is a natural next source of evidence that does not depend on the history. Code
and trained selectors will be released.
